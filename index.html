The brief

11/83 Hector St W
Osborne Park, WA

What I was selling

The Bodyweight Gym was Perth's first dedicated calisthenics and bodyweight gym. It ran out of a unit on Hector Street in Osborne Park. There were no weight machines. Members trained on rings, bars and floor space, working toward a first pull-up, a handstand, a muscle-up.

That is a specific marketing problem. In 2017 most people in Perth had not heard the word calisthenics, and the ones who had assumed it was for gymnasts. Nobody was searching for us. We could not beat the franchises on price or opening hours, and we did not want to sell on before-and-after photos.

So the job had two halves: explain what the gym did in language an ordinary person recognised, and give that person a low-risk reason to walk in and try it. Almost everything below is the second half.

Membership drive events

You cannot sell a gym membership cold to someone who has never done the training. So I stopped trying to, and sold an event instead.

Every few months I ran a membership drive built around a free open session at the gym. One date, one offer, something a person could say yes to without committing to anything. I planned the format with the coaches, wrote and designed the promotion, ran it out across Facebook and Instagram in the lead-up, handled the enquiries and registrations, and was there on the day.

The events did two things a discount never does. They got people physically inside the room, where the coaching and the other members do the selling for you. And they gave the campaign something real to talk about, which is far easier to write than "join our gym".

The standing offer sat behind all of it: two weeks free, nothing up front. The event was the reason to start the two weeks.

Mum’s n Bubs

Mum’s n Bubs came out of a gap I could see from inside the business: parents of small children who wanted to train, and had nowhere to put the kids.

I built it as a product rather than another line on the timetable. That meant deciding who it was for, what actually happened in the room, when it ran, what it cost, and how a parent at home with a toddler would ever hear about it. The children got a cubby in the corner of the gym to draw in, and Corey coached the class.

The interesting problem turned out to be the name. Parents kept assuming it meant using the baby as a weight, and that it was for mothers only. It was neither. It was an ordinary class you could bring your kids to without feeling watched, and fathers came as well. A good deal of the promotion was simply correcting that, in plain language, as often as it took.

It ran as a regular part of the timetable and brought in members the gym would not otherwise have reached. It also made the gym visibly a place for people who were not already fit, which was the harder problem.

The Push-Up Challenge

The Push-Up Challenge is a Perth-founded mental health campaign. Over about three weeks each year, participants work through a target number of push-ups — 3,046 in 2020, 3,318 in 2021 — matching the lives lost to suicide in Australia in the reporting year, and raise money for mental health services.

It belonged at our gym. Mental health was already one of the things we posted about most, and not as a gesture: R U OK? Day ran as a proper piece of the calendar, and the theme came back through the year in its own template.

I registered the gym, promoted it to members and to the public, ran it as an open community event rather than a members-only one, and built that month's content around it.

It raised money for a cause our community already cared about, and it put the gym in front of people who would never have clicked a fitness ad.

The identity

I built the gym's identity and kept it consistent across the shopfront, the website, the timetable, the class names, the merchandise and every post that went out. One blue, one logo, one place the logo sits, and a set of layouts that a post could be dropped into without starting from scratch.

That last part is the unglamorous half of a small-business brand. A thousand posts is only possible if the design work is systematic. Coach introductions, class promotions, technique explainers and quote cards each had their own template, so the account stayed recognisable at volume and a post took minutes rather than an afternoon.

The brand had one job beyond that: make a technical and slightly intimidating discipline look like somewhere an unfit beginner could walk in on a Tuesday night. Most gyms in the category market to people who are already strong. We led with the coaches and the members instead of with physiques.

Facebook and Instagram

Facebook and Instagram were the gym's primary channels for four years. I ran both: planning the content, shooting it in the gym, writing it, managing the community, and running the paid campaigns behind events and intakes.

The Instagram account published 1,007 posts, to an audience of roughly two thousand there and thirteen hundred on Facebook. For a single-location studio in Osborne Park that is a real local audience, and it was built a post at a time.

What went out

  • Coaches Every coach got a written introduction, so a new member knew who they would be training with before they arrived.
  • Members Real members at the stage they were actually at, rather than models. It had its own archive on the profile.
  • Technique Progressions and explainers that gave people a reason to follow the account whether or not they ever joined.
  • Health & mindset R U OK? Day, and a steady line through the year about training for your head as much as your body.
  • The timetable Class times, formats and coaches, kept current and easy to find. The most useful thing on the account.
  • Perth #perthfitfam, #perthisok, #perthcalisthenics. A local gym has to be findable by locals.

Off the feed I ran a members-only Facebook group, so the community had somewhere to talk to each other, find training partners and post their own training. Reach was never the point on its own. The point was that a small gym should feel like somewhere you already belonged before you walked in.

Closing the doors

COVID closed the gym. Western Australia shut gyms in late March 2020 and allowed them to reopen that June. The doors opened again, but a small studio that depends on people training together in a room does not recover quickly from a year like that, and the Osborne Park premises closed in 2021.

Two things about that period are worth saying as marketing work.

The first is that we did not go quiet. In November 2020 we launched The Bodyweight Gym Online and put the classes into a web app, free while it was in development, taught by the coaches members already knew. Holding a community together when you cannot open the doors is a communications job, and it is the one I am most proud of.

The second is that winding a business down in public is also a marketing job, and a harder one. You owe people a straight explanation and somewhere to go next. The last thing the website said was this:

We have closed - thankyou to all our members that made the gym what it was!

The gym still exists. It is online only now, at thebodyweightgym.org ↗, where the class library is free.
